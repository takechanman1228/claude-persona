## Preference Verdict

Fragmented, with a narrow edge for Concept C (4/10) over Concepts A and B (3/10 each) — no concept reaches majority support. However, C's choosers carry the panel's strongest purchase intent (mean 3.75 vs. 2.67 for A choosers and 2.33 for B choosers), making C the leading concept for actual conversion despite the split vote.

## Key Findings

1. **The vote splits on outcome vs. exit.** C's four choosers (Karim, Thibault, Aissatou, Ines) all picked it because it's "the only one selling a result instead of an exit door" — a concrete promise about weeknight dinners. A and B's six choosers picked those concepts precisely because they prioritize low-commitment terms; several explicitly distrusted C's "noticeably easier within a week" as an unverifiable claim.

2. **Purchase intent tracks with the outcome promise, not the safety framing.** Every persona rating 4/5 either chose C or (Elodie) chose A while still demanding verifiable terms. B's choosers are the panel's least likely buyers (2, 2, 3): they preferred B because it "admits the trap exists," which validates skepticism rather than converting it — B wins agreement, not purchases.

3. **The subscription model itself is the biggest single barrier.** Three personas across all three segments (Margaux, Julien, Gerard — purchase likelihood 2, 2, 1) said the fix is not messaging but removing the subscription entirely in favor of single-box, on-demand purchase.

4. **Anti-anxiety framing backfires with confident cooks and operators.** Multiple personas read B's fear-reduction language as condescending or manipulative — Ines called it "marketing to my anxieties instead of solving anything," and Thibault said it "was written for someone scared of food." Fear-led copy filters out exactly the high-energy weeknight users C attracts.

5. **C's promise is believed conditionally, not on faith.** Even C's choosers demanded proof mechanisms — honest cooking times (Aissatou), kid-tested meals (Ines), measurable first-week improvement (Karim, Ines). C converts if the product substantiates the claim quickly; it collapses if week one resembles "recipes that claim 20 minutes and take 50."

## Segment / Profile Splits

- **Families With Young Children (N=3)** → lean C (2/3). Shared driver: the 18h30–18h45 weeknight crunch with tired kids is their concrete pain, and only C addresses it. The one defector (Julien) rejects subscriptions categorically, not C specifically.
- **Dual-Income Couples Without Children (N=4)** → fully split (C:2, A:1, B:1). Younger, energy-poor couples (Karim, Thibault) chose C for the weeknight result; the process-oriented (Elodie) and category-skeptical (Margaux) members chose on terms transparency and honesty of framing instead.
- **Independent Professionals & Empty Nesters (N=3)** → lean A (2/3), and notably **zero votes for C**. Shared reason: nobody in this segment feels weeknight time pressure ("nobody is rushing me anywhere" — Gerard), so exit flexibility and low-pressure entry are the only appeals. Also the lowest-intent segment overall (3, 3, 1).

## Purchase-Intent Drivers

Intent rose where a concept matched a felt weeknight pain and where terms felt verifiable: the four highest scores (all 4s) came from personas who either bought into C's outcome promise or, like Elodie, saw upfront skip/cancel terms as a trust signal worth acting on. Intent fell on three recurring factors: subscription lock-in and card-on-file requirements (Julien, Gerard, Margaux), opaque or per-portion-hidden pricing (Elodie, Thibault, Baptiste), and claim credibility — "enjoyable within a week" was repeatedly flagged as marketing copy rather than a spec. Food waste for small households (Sylvie's "half a courgette dying in the fridge door") was a distinct depressor for solo eaters.

## Per-Concept Strengths and Weaknesses

### Concept C — 4/10 chose this
**Strengths (cross-persona):** Only concept selling a tangible outcome, and it maps precisely onto the weeknight-dinner crisis that time-poor workers and parents describe as their real problem. Ines and Aissatou both noted C addresses "my actual problem" while A and B talk about leaving. Its choosers show the panel's highest intent (3.75 mean) and stress-test willingness — Karim framed a two-week trial as "the cost of one bad restaurant."
**Weaknesses (cross-persona):** The one-week promise strains credibility with every non-chooser and even some choosers. Margaux, Sylvie, Elodie, and Baptiste independently dismissed it as unprovable ad copy ("the sort of claim I'd strike from a client's prospectus" — Sylvie). Zero traction with empty nesters, who feel no weeknight urgency.

### Concept A — 3/10 chose this
**Strengths (cross-persona):** Upfront skip-or-cancel terms function as a trust signal for burned subscribers (Elodie) and a viable low-pressure entry for chaotic-schedule beginners (Baptiste). It's the least objectionable concept — even non-choosers called it merely "beige" rather than off-putting.
**Weaknesses (cross-persona):** "Easy to cancel" is table stakes ("every app has skip and cancel now" — Karim) and can backfire by implying cancellation is normally hard (Julien). "Straightforward meals" reads as bland to flavor-driven cooks, and A attracts low-intent choosers (Gerard at 1/5 chose it only as the easiest to leave).

### Concept B — 3/10 chose this
**Strengths (cross-persona):** Its honesty about money and food waste earns respect from the panel's skeptics — Margaux, Julien, and Sylvie all credited B with naming the category's real flaw rather than papering over it. Sylvie called it "the only honest pitch of the three."
**Weaknesses (cross-persona):** Leading with fear repels confident users — Ines, Thibault, and Baptiste all found the anxiety framing condescending or suspicious ("if you have to lead with fear, that worries me" — Elodie). Critically, B validates objections without resolving them: all three choosers scored 3 or below on purchase intent.

## Improvement Theme Clusters

- **Radical terms transparency** (4 mentions across A, B, C) — publish full cancellation/skip conditions and exact price per portion on the landing page, pre-account; Elodie ("no asterisks, no 'from €X' games") and Sylvie both made this their precondition to trial.
- **Kill or bypass the subscription** (3 mentions across A, B) — sell single boxes on demand, no account or card on file; Julien and Gerard framed this as the only model they'd consider, Margaux as the fix for wilting unwanted vegetables.
- **Portion & price fit for small/solo households** (3 mentions across A, B, C) — generous single portions (Sylvie), 1–2 person student pricing under ~€40/week (Baptiste), and split-portion spice levels within a couple (Karim).
- **Bold, authentic flavor options** (2 mentions, Concept C) — Karim and Thibault both warned that timid, under-seasoned "international" recipes would drive churn by week two; they want real spice, garlic, and fresh herbs as configurable defaults.
- **Family-real features** (2 mentions, Concept C) — kid-sized portions, picky-eater handling, holiday-synced skips (Ines); honest kid-acceptability of meals (Aissatou).
- **Honest time claims** (1 mention, Concept C, but echoed in skepticism from Elodie) — state real cooking time, "not 'chef time'" (Aissatou), as the proof point C's promise depends on.
