## Preference Verdict

**Clear winner: Concept B**, with 7 of 10 personas (70%) versus 3 for Concept A (30%) and 0 for Concept C. The margin is decisive, though enthusiasm behind B is moderate rather than hot — several B-choosers picked it as "least objectionable" (Kenji at likelihood 2) rather than compelling. Concept C received zero votes and drew actively hostile reactions across the panel.

## Key Findings

1. **The panel split cleanly on "what is the real problem: writing minutes, or decisions evaporating?"** The 7 B-choosers — spanning managers, compliance roles, and small-business owners — consistently framed follow-through as the pain ("decisions dissolve after everyone leaves the room" — Sachiko; "half-melted like April snow" by the time HQ decisions reach stores — Emi). The 3 A-choosers were individual contributors for whom the writing itself is the billable/evening-consuming burden.
2. **Concept C was universally rejected as manager surveillance.** Eight of ten personas volunteered negative reactions to C without being asked, using words like "watching," "surveillance," and "dashboard for headquarters." Naomi (HR) articulated the mechanism: once employees know calls feed a visibility dashboard, "the honest sentences move to the hallway afterward" — you buy an archive of tatemae, not truth.
3. **"Save time" is a dead claim in this category.** Multiple personas across both camps dismissed A's efficiency framing as commoditized ("what every AI notes tool already says" — Yui; "every tool says" that — Daisuke). Even A-choosers picked it for a specific job (eliminating non-billable minutes hours), not for the generic promise.
4. **Data residency and security documentation are hard gates, not features, for institutional buyers.** The two lowest purchase-likelihood scores (Kenji and Hiroshi, both 2) came from personas who conditioned any evaluation on domestic data storage, non-use of audio for training, and Japanese-language security documentation — regardless of which concept they preferred.
5. **Automation output must survive Japanese formal review to have value.** Personas whose minutes are legal, contractual, or client deliverables (Akiko, Takumi, Sachiko, Kenji) stressed that a transcript that mangles Japanese business terminology or lacks proper gijiroku formatting saves nothing — they'd re-edit anyway.

## Segment / Profile Splits

*Note: each segment is N=1; patterns below group segments by shared logic, not statistical weight.*

- **Institutional gatekeepers** (Paper-First Bucho, Security Gatekeeper, Compliance-First Director) → **B**, but with low-to-moderate intent (2–3) — B is the only defensible framing before a security committee or accreditation body, yet all three gate purchase on data handling and auditability.
- **Team-outcome managers and owners** (Honne-Nuance Skeptic, Onsite Ops Realist, Cost-Counting Owner) → **B** — decisions-and-follow-through directly maps to their accountability problem; all explicitly rejected C's top-down visibility logic.
- **Individual doers drowning in write-ups** (Solo Efficiency Buyer, Minutes-Drowned PM, Voice-Memo Rookie) → **A** — the document itself is the bottleneck; follow-ups they can already manage themselves.
- **Bottom-up tool adopter** (Free-Tier Power User) → **B** — an IC exception: her pain is promised follow-ups vanishing into Slack, not typing.

## Purchase-Intent Drivers

The three 4-scores (Yui, Akiko, Ryo) all came from personas who could self-adopt without organizational approval and saw a direct personal-workflow payoff — billable hours recovered, evenings freed, follow-ups auto-chased. Intent was suppressed by unresolved trust questions: the two 2-scores (Kenji, Hiroshi) trace to data residency, audio-training use, and record-integrity concerns rather than to the value proposition itself. Across the panel, per-seat dollar pricing, absence of a free tier, and doubts about Japanese transcription accuracy were the most-cited friction points holding otherwise-favorable personas at 3.

## Per-Concept Strengths and Weaknesses

### Concept B — 7/10 chose this
**Strengths (cross-persona):** Frames the meeting's purpose correctly — decisions and follow-through, not the record for its own sake. This resonated with accountability-driven roles: Sachiko (accreditation records), Daisuke (scope-dispute protection), Emi (HQ-to-store execution), and Hiroshi noted it's "the one benefit I could defend in front of the security committee." Naomi credited B with confining itself "to outcomes rather than utterances," sidestepping the recording-chills-honesty problem.
**Weaknesses (cross-persona):** Wins partly by default — Kenji called it "least objectionable" and scored 2. A-choosers (Takumi, Ryo) found follow-up tracking redundant with existing PM tools or their own memory. B does not yet answer the security, data-residency, and access-control questions that its own institutional supporters require before purchase.

### Concept A — 3/10 chose this
**Strengths (cross-persona):** Directly attacks a quantifiable pain for individual contributors: Takumi loses 2–3 hours of minutes-writing per two-hour meeting; Akiko bills against eliminated non-billable hours; Ryo's evenings go to visit reports. For these users, the writing — not the remembering — is the bottleneck.
**Weaknesses (cross-persona):** The "records automatically / saves time" claim is commoditized and triggered skepticism among passers (Yui, Daisuke). "Records everything" raised works-council and data-residency alarms (Naomi, Hiroshi, Kenji). Even choosers conditioned value on transcription quality for specialized Japanese terminology — if the transcript is garbage, "I'm editing anyway and saving nothing" (Akiko).

### Concept C — 0/10 chose this
**Strengths (cross-persona):** None credited by any persona.
**Weaknesses (cross-persona):** Read as surveillance software for bosses across nearly the entire panel — ICs (Yui, Ryo) felt watched; managers themselves (Emi, Daisuke, Kenji) rejected dashboards as a substitute for direct presence; Naomi flagged it as destroying candor; Hiroshi and Sachiko noted the CRM/pipeline framing simply doesn't map to government or healthcare contexts and creates labor-relations exposure. C should be shelved or fundamentally reframed.

## Improvement Theme Clusters

- **Security documentation & domestic data residency** (4 mentions across A, B) — Japanese-language security checklists, domestic/on-premise or LGWAN deployment, non-use of audio for training, approved-tool lists (Hiroshi, Kenji, Takumi, Sachiko).
- **Japanese-language quality & formal formats** (5 mentions across A, B) — accurate transcription of business/technical Japanese, government and stampable gijiroku templates, native-quality Japanese UI, clean Word/Excel export (Akiko, Takumi, Daisuke, Yui, Kenji).
- **Japan-market pricing** (4 mentions across A, B) — flat yen pricing for solo/small teams, a free tier for bottom-up trial, one-time purchase options instead of per-seat dollar subscriptions (Akiko, Daisuke, Yui, Kenji).
- **Privacy controls & recording minimization** (2 mentions, B) — granular access/retention controls, non-recorded meeting flags, and a mode capturing decisions without keeping raw audio (Naomi, Sachiko).
- **Mobile voice-first capture** (2 mentions across A, B) — hands-free operation from a car or phone voice memos, with follow-ups auto-routed and CRM fields auto-filled (Ryo, Emi).
- **Workflow integrations** (2 mentions, B/A) — push follow-up reminders into Slack; auto-populate CRM visit-report fields (Yui, Ryo).
