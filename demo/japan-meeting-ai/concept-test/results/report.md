## Preference Verdict

**Clear winner: Concept B** (9 of 10 personas, 90%). Concept A received one vote from a solo field-worker persona; Concept C received zero votes. The margin is decisive and spans every occupational category and organizational scale in the panel. However, the panel-wide purchase likelihood mean of 3.2 (max observed: 4) signals that strong preference does not automatically translate into near-term purchase intent — a meaningful gap that implementation and compliance concerns explain.

---

## Key Findings

1. **"Decision decay" is the universal pain point, and Concept B owns it.** Personas across wildly different contexts — a startup COO, a government section chief, a freelance designer, a factory GM — converged on the same underlying problem: decisions made in meetings evaporate before anyone acts on them. B's positioning around post-meeting accountability and follow-up ownership hit this nerve directly, making it feel like a solution to a recognized crisis rather than an efficiency nice-to-have.

2. **Concept A was rejected as table stakes, not as a bad product.** The panel did not dislike A; they considered it baseline infrastructure. Haruka (DX Promotion Lead) articulated what many implied — "every tool in this category promises time savings, and that's not a differentiator anymore." Even Shota, the sole A voter, chose it for a highly personal, individual reason (eliminating two hours of nightly report-writing), not because the concept's core value proposition stood out competitively.

3. **Concept C was disqualified by context mismatch, not by feature quality.** All nine B-choosers flagged C as built for sales teams managing CRM pipelines. Not a single non-sales persona could map it onto their daily reality. This is a positioning and targeting failure — C may be viable in a different audience segment, but as presented to this panel it was eliminated on relevance grounds alone before any deeper evaluation occurred.

4. **Compliance and data governance are the primary ceiling on purchase likelihood.** Four personas (Rina, Takeshi, Sachiko, Noboru) explicitly cited data residency, security certification, and privacy concerns as the reason their purchase intent did not exceed 3 — even when they preferred B. Concept A's audio-recording feature raised additional concerns for personas handling confidential financial or client data. Without credible answers on data handling, preference for B cannot convert to purchase.

5. **The solo A vote reveals an underserved individual-contributor use case.** Shota's preference for A is not an outlier to dismiss — it points to a distinct segment (field workers who generate high volumes of solo or one-on-one encounters and need frictionless mobile capture) that none of the three concepts serve well. B's team-accountability framing is largely irrelevant when there is no team in the room.

## Segment / Profile Splits

**Enterprise and regulated-sector personas** (Overwhelmed Section Chief, Skeptical IT Gatekeeper, Cost-Conscious SMB Owner, Government Bureaucrat) → all chose **B**, but purchase likelihood averaged 2.5 → shared reason: accountability framing resonates, but procurement and security review requirements are genuine blockers, not negotiating tactics.

**Growth-oriented professionals** (Startup Juggler, Efficiency-Obsessed Manager, Innovation Champion, Remote Creative Freelancer) → unanimously chose **B**, purchase likelihood averaged 4.0 → shared reason: they experience decision-execution gaps daily, have authority to adopt tools without lengthy review cycles, and can immediately picture B in their workflow.

**Analog or change-resistant personas** (Analog Traditionalist, Government Bureaucrat) → chose **B** on concept merit, purchase likelihood averaged 2.0 → shared reason: B solved a recognizable problem, but adoption skepticism and institutional inertia suppress intent regardless of concept quality. Note: both are N=1 segments; do not over-generalize.

**Individual contributor / solo worker** (Young Field Rep) → chose **A**, purchase likelihood 4 → B's team-accountability framing was irrelevant; daily personal admin burden was the only problem worth solving. This is a single-persona signal — directional but not generalizable without further research.

## Purchase-Intent Drivers

Across the panel, the factors that elevated purchase likelihood to 4 were: (1) a direct, immediately recognizable personal pain the concept solved without requiring organizational buy-in (clearest in Shota, Yuki, and Ayaka), and (2) confidence that existing workflow tools — Slack, Jira, Teams, Zoom — would not be disrupted but augmented. What suppressed intent to 2–3 was not concept skepticism but institutional friction: data residency ambiguity (raised by Rina, Takeshi, Sachiko), absence of Japan-region hosting confirmation, and lack of evidence that procurement or security review could be satisfied. No persona reported that price or the feature set itself was the limiting factor.

## Per-Concept Strengths and Weaknesses

### Concept B — 9/10 chose this
**Strengths (cross-persona):** B's framing around decision accountability and follow-up ownership resonated across every organizational type, from a 12-person startup to a prefectural government division. The concept addressed a problem personas already felt acutely, making it easy to self-identify as the target user without prompting. Haruka praised its internal pitch clarity ("reduces decision decay"), and Kenji valued it as "accountability infrastructure" rather than transcription polish. Even skeptical or low-intent personas like Masahiro and Noboru acknowledged it addressed something concrete and real.

**Weaknesses (cross-persona):** The concept as presented gave security and compliance-minded evaluators nothing to work with. Takeshi described the current state as "three incomplete descriptions," and Rina could not progress without data residency documentation. Additionally, B's positioning is team-centric, which rendered it less compelling for solo or field-based workers — Shota found it "more like a team coordination tool" — suggesting a potential coverage gap for individual-contributor buyers.

---

### Concept A — 1/10 chose this
**Strengths (cross-persona):** Auto-recording and automatic summary generation is universally understood as useful, and Shota's high purchase likelihood (4) demonstrates that for high-volume solo-meeting workers, the time-recovery value proposition is viscerally compelling. The concept is easy to grasp and has an obvious daily use case for field roles.

**Weaknesses (cross-persona):** "Table stakes" framing was a recurring dismissal. Multiple personas (Haruka, Yuki, Rina) noted that time savings on admin are no longer differentiating in this product category. Separately, the audio-recording feature triggered compliance concerns among those handling confidential or financial meeting content — Rina explicitly said it would not pass a financial institution security review, and Sachiko flagged the same concern. These are not edge objections; they are structural barriers for a substantial portion of the potential buyer base.

---

### Concept C — 0/10 chose this
**Strengths (cross-persona):** No persona offered genuine praise for C; it was eliminated on fit grounds before strengths were evaluated.

**Weaknesses (cross-persona):** Across all nine B-choosers who commented on C, the verdict was the same: it was built for sales teams managing customer pipelines, and it had no relevance to internal meetings, cross-functional planning, field visits, or government records. The CRM and "manager visibility" framing actively alienated non-sales personas — Shota interpreted it as "the boss checking up on me." Concept C may have merit in a dedicated sales-tech evaluation, but it was a category mismatch for this panel.

## Improvement Theme Clusters

- **Data residency, security certification, and compliance documentation** (5 mentions across B, A — Rina, Takeshi, Sachiko, Noboru, Shota implied) — Multiple personas cannot advance to procurement evaluation without Japan-region hosting confirmation, SOC 2 / ISO 27001 certification, and on-premise or private cloud deployment options; Takeshi called this a prerequisite, not a preference.

- **Native integration with existing collaboration tools** (3 mentions across B — Kenji, Yuki, Haruka) — Action items should push directly into Jira/Backlog, Slack threads, Teams, or Zoom without requiring context-switching into a separate app; the gap between "noted in meeting tool" and "assigned in task system" was cited as the exact failure mode these personas need solved.

- **Japanese language fidelity and localization** (2 mentions across B — Haruka, Sachiko) — If the AI cannot accurately parse keigo, implied decisions, and technical business vocabulary in Japanese, the core value proposition collapses in enterprise Japan settings; Sachiko also requires a fully Japanese-language UI for staff adoption.

- **Mobile-first, low-friction capture for field use** (1 mention, Concept A — Shota) — A tap-to-record, summary-on-mobile experience is essential for field workers who never return to a desk between appointments; laptop-centric design disqualifies the product for this use case. Note: single-persona signal only.

- **Multi-project / multi-client follow-up dashboards** (1 mention, Concept B — Ayaka) — Freelancers and consultants managing three or more concurrent client relationships need a consolidated view of outstanding actions across projects, not just per-meeting summaries.

- **Empirical ROI evidence and output verifiability** (2 mentions across B — Masahiro, Noboru) — Skeptical and process-governed personas require measurable proof (e.g., missed follow-ups caught over 90 days on a real deployment) and human review checkpoints before AI outputs can be treated as authoritative records; vendor claims without data are dismissed outright.

## Caveats

- Virtual panel of 10 personas; findings are directional only
- Each segment is represented by a single persona — segment-level conclusions should not be over-generalized; use as hypothesis input for quantitative follow-up
- AI-generated responses may exhibit positivity bias; purchase likelihood scores and stated objections should be weighted accordingly
- Concept descriptions were not reproduced in the data; inferences about A, B, and C are derived entirely from persona reasoning and may not fully reflect the actual concept stimuli
